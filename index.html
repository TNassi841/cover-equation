The Cover Equation

The equation says one simple thing: the change right now is what arrives minus what leaves. The twist is that what arrives was decided a while ago. That late reaction is what draws the shape on the thesis cover.

The cover

Four dials

Move one and the picture redraws. Each dial says what the cover uses.

τCover: 17. How old the news is.
βCover: 0.2. How hard new supply pushes in.
nCover: 10. How suddenly supply gets cut when there is too much.
γCover: 0.1. The share that disappears over one unit of time.

Arrivals against departures

Blue-green is what arrives for each amount. The red line is what leaves. Where they cross, the two cancel and nothing would change. That crossing is the balance point.

The shaded band shows where the amount really swings at these settings. At the cover's settings the crossing sits on the steep, falling side of the hump. A small rise in the amount causes a big cut in supply, and that cut shows up one delay later, which is why it keeps overshooting.

Each term in plain words

Picture x as the number of cells in the blood. New cells take time to mature, and old ones die off. The buttons change the dials so you can see the result.

ẋ(t)

The change right now

The dot means speed. Positive means the amount is rising, negative means it is falling. The whole equation is a rule for this one number, used again and again.

On the picture: every use of the rule places one dot. The cover has 29,660 of them.

x(t−τ)

The amount one delay ago

The system reacts to old news. Cells arriving today were started by what the body looked like τ earlier.

On the picture: this is the star of the equation. A short delay lets the system correct itself and the shape shrinks to a point. A longer delay makes it overshoot, so loops appear. Longer still, and the loops stop lining up.

β

Arrival strength

How much new supply comes in for a given amount. Think of it as the volume knob on production.

On the picture: turn it up and the swings get bigger. Below γ, supply cannot keep up and everything fades to zero.

1 / (1 + xn)

The brake, set by n

With few cells, this part is close to 1 and has no effect, so more cells means more new cells. With too many, the bottom gets huge and supply collapses. Together that makes the hump in the chart above. With the cover's n of 10, the hump peaks near x = 0.8.

On the picture: n is how sharply the brake grabs. A soft brake gives a calm system with no loops. A sharp one makes hard swings. Sharper does not always mean wilder: at n = 15 the pattern tidies up again.

γ x(t)

What leaves

A fixed share of whatever is there right now disappears. This part has no delay at all. It is the steady drain that pulls the amount back down.

On the picture: more drain lowers the balance point. At 0.15 the shape calms to one small loop. Too much and everything dies out.

How a line becomes this shape

  1. The equation produces one wiggly line over time, shown under the picture.
  2. At each moment, read two numbers from that line: the amount now and the amount one delay ago.
  3. Use those two numbers as the position of one dot. Now sets left and right, one delay ago sets up and down.
  4. Where the line moves slowly the dots are large and dark. Where it moves fast they are small and faint. That is why some edges look inked in.
  5. The color only shows the order: teal is early, indigo is the middle, terracotta is late.

Press Play to watch the dot trace its path. For the cover, the shape is also tilted by about 19 degrees and the up and down direction is flipped. Both are only for looks.

What this picture is not

This is artwork. It uses a classic equation and is separate from the breathing model in Chapter 6 of the thesis. The shape says nothing about any patient.

Mackey and Glass's 1977 paper covered both breathing control and blood cell production. This form is the one usually tied to blood cells, which is why the example here uses cells.
